I’m a planner, probably to a fault, which isn’t the obvious start for someone writing about slow travel. Before I head anywhere I’m studying maps and reading up on the area, looking for something different or a bit special, because the time I spend outside is what brings me calm and connects me to the world, and I want to get the most out of it.

The trouble with planning that much is that a trip rarely goes exactly the way I pictured it. For a long time, when it didn’t, it got to me. I’d come home sad or annoyed, as if whatever did happen wasn’t enough. That’s where slow travel comes in.

Slowing down and paying attention to the smaller things has helped with that, and it’s helped my photography too. As well as the views, I notice the light and how it changes through the day, coming through the trees or breaking through cloud to light up a small patch of the glen.

Sunrise feels different. The cloud often sits much lower, and I like watching it lift and move across the hills and through the trees, especially when it catches some colour. Sunset is warmer, and the hour after it, when everything turns a shade of blue, is my favourite time of day. The warmth has gone and the light is flat, but it’s calm.

Colour turns up everywhere, in surprising tones. Look at the sky long enough at either end of the day and you start to see past the grey cloud or the plain blue to oranges, pinks and peach, with light greens and yellows around the edges, then later dark purples and blues.

Then there’s the sound of different trees moving in the wind in spring and summer, frost on autumn mornings, a hoar frost in winter, spiders’ webs in the long grass and the small insects flying around my feet as I walk past. And there’s a fire in the evening, the owls, and the eyes of deer lit up in the distance.

That’s why I started Slow Scotland. I still spend hours looking at maps, but now I share those places so other people can slow down and feel it too.
